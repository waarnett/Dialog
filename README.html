Dialog

A moderated group conversation between you and several AI models: Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, DeepSeek or any other model OpenRouter offers, and models running in LM Studio on your own computer. You decide who speaks next. Every participant sees the same conversation, except for private exchanges you have with one or more of them and your asides to the readers, which no participant sees. Dialog is a single web page; Safari and Chrome both work.

Getting started

  1. Get an OpenRouter API key. The participants speak through OpenRouter, which passes your messages to each model's maker and bills you for what they use.

    1. Go to openrouter.ai and sign up (or sign in), with Google, GitHub or an email address.
    2. On the Credits page, add a little credit with a card; $10 lasts a good while. OpenRouter adds a 5.5% fee to each purchase, and unused credit expires after a year.
    3. On the Keys page, create a key. Name it Dialog, and give it a credit limit (for instance $10 a month): it caps what Dialog can ever spend, and can reset daily, weekly or monthly. You can change the limit later on the same page.
    4. Copy the key (it starts sk-or-v1-) as soon as it is shown: OpenRouter shows it only once. If you lose it, delete it on the Keys page and create another.

  2. Open Dialog: online at https://waarnett.github.io/Dialog/, or double-click a copy of index.html (keep README.html beside it).

  3. Click Settings (top right), paste the key into OpenRouter API key, and put in your name (until you do, the participants call you Grasshopper) and, if you like, a few sentences about you. Look over the house rules, the instructions every participant gets. Click Done. The key is kept only in this browser on this computer, and sent only to OpenRouter.

    What you're spending. From then on the top bar shows what this conversation has cost and what the key has spent this month (see The top bar, below). To see your remaining credit there too (optional), create a management key on OpenRouter's Management Keys page, with an expiration date, and paste it into OpenRouter management key in Settings. Keep it safe: it can create new keys, so anyone who got hold of it could spend your credit.

  4. Participants. The first time you open Dialog, two are already seated, under Participants on the right: Claude, the latest Claude Opus, and Gemini, the latest Gemini Pro. Each always gets the newest model in its family, as OpenRouter releases them. To add more, press + Add a seat and choose a model. Add as many as you like, including several of the same model, and give each one a role if you want it to play a particular part (see Roles, below).

  5. Talk. Type a message and end it with a name or with "team": "What's the strongest objection to this, team?" (see Talking, below).

No OpenRouter account? With models in LM Studio on your own computer you can do without one: leave the key empty and see Models on this computer (LM Studio), below.

Until the conversation starts, a welcome screen lists the steps still to do, links to this guide and shows Dialog's version number.

The top bar

  • Dialog and the Socrates medallion: the version number shows under the name after you reload the page or start a new conversation, and goes with the next turn. Click either to go through the version number, a link to this guide in its place, and nothing; what you click to stays through later turns. This guide shows the version number under its own Dialog at the top.
  • This conversation: what its replies have cost, as OpenRouter reports it. OpenRouter's 5.5% fee on credit purchases comes on top.
  • Credit left (only with a management key; see Settings): your account's remaining credit. Hover for credit bought and used; click to open OpenRouter's Credits page.
  • This month: what this key has spent since the 1st (months begin at midnight UTC). Hover for its all-time spending and its limit.
  • Save All or Save Selected, Restore… (only while the conversation is empty), New conversation and Settings: see Saving, restoring and tabs, and Settings.

A notice under the top bar says when something needs attention: no API key yet, or the browser refusing to let Dialog save (the conversation would then be lost when the page closes).

Participants

Each participant (a "seat") has a card on the right:

  • Name, in the seat's color: click it to rename. It is what the others see ("Skeptic says: …") and what appears in the conversation's margin. The logo of the model's maker follows it.
  • The model button opens the model picker: every OpenRouter model with its price (dollars per million tokens, input / output), plus LM Studio's models if you use them. Type a few words ("opus", "gemini pro") to narrow the list; Sort orders it by name or price; click a model or use the arrow keys and Return. A full model id (containing a "/") can be typed too. Refresh list fetches the newest list at once, for a model that has only just appeared; the line under the list says how old it is.
  • Under the button, the model's id and price, or a warning that the id is no longer in OpenRouter's list. Ids beginning with "~" (such as "~google/gemini-pro-latest") always follow a family's newest model, and the id shown is the one they stand for now.
  • Thinking: how hard the model thinks before replying, offering exactly the levels the model supports, with its default marked. Thinking is billed as output, whether or not you get to read it.
  • Role: the participant's standing instructions; see Roles, below. It shows "(set)" or "(none)".
  • Sleep benches the participant without removing it. Its card folds up and moves to the end of the list. A sleeping participant sits out "team" rounds and isn't the one Return asks by default, but still replies when you name it: it wakes for that one reply and goes back to sleep after it. Untick Sleep and it returns to its place.
  • Speak asks the participant to reply now; Stop ends its reply early. While it speaks, its card is outlined.
  • ⠿ (left of the name) moves the card: drag it, or click it and press ↑ or ↓. The order is the order of the To menu and of "team" rounds.
  • + copy adds another seat with the same model and settings, but no role. × removes the seat; its earlier contributions stay in the conversation.
  • The participant's cost so far is at the bottom right of the card.

Web search and page reading are always on for models that can use them; the card says so when a model can't.

Roles

A role turns a model into a particular participant. "Claude" and "Claude-Skeptic" can run the same model; what makes them different is mostly the role.

What a role is. Any text you write in the seat's Role box. That participant starts with it, after the house rules and About you, as "Your role in this conversation". Because it comes last, a role can override the house rules for that one participant.

What a role can do:

  • Give a point of view or a position: "Argue the skeptic's side. Find the weakest step in each argument and press on it." Two seats with opposite positions make a debate.
  • Give a job: fact-checker, editor, summarizer, devil's advocate, note-taker. "When I ask, summarize where the group agrees, where it disagrees and what is still open, in under 200 words."
  • Give a manner or a voice: blunt, patient, Socratic (answer with questions), a teacher explaining to a beginner, a historian who separates what is known from what is guessed.
  • Set the form of its replies: length, structure, language, level of detail.
  • Override a house rule for this participant only: "For this role, ignore the rule on length: write as much as the question needs." Or "Don't give confidence percentages."
  • Narrow its attention: "Look only at the numbers: check every figure anyone gives."

What a role can't do:

  • Add knowledge or abilities. "You are the world's leading expert on X" adds no expertise; it mostly makes the model sound more sure of itself. Say instead what to focus on and what to check.
  • Tell the others anything. A role is seen only by its own participant. The others see its name and model, and what it says. If they should know its part, say so in a message, or let the name tell them ("Skeptic").
  • Make it speak up on its own, or decide who goes next: you do, every turn.
  • Show it what it can't see: other participants' private exchanges, their thinking, or earlier conversations. Each participant knows only the conversation as it sees it.
  • Change how Dialog works: how messages are labeled, who sees what, or how files are made.
  • Get past the model's own limits. What its maker trained it to refuse, it still refuses.
  • Guarantee obedience. In a long conversation, or when a role pulls against the model's habits, a participant can drift out of it. Remind it in a message, or make the role plainer and firmer.

Changing a role takes effect on the participant's next turn; its earlier replies stay as they were. The change goes into the conversation as a stage direction that only you and that participant see (see Stage directions, below).

Roles are saved with the conversation (a page saved with Save All starts each participant's line with the first sentence of its role, and its triangle opens the whole role) and with the settings (Save settings to a file).

Role, house rules or About you? The house rules are for every participant; a role is for one. About you is what everyone should know about you.

Talking

Adding a message and asking for a reply

  • Return adds your message and asks a participant to reply, so a back-and-forth needs no clicking. The words at the end of your message say who (see below).
  • ⌘↩ (Command-Return; Ctrl+Enter on other systems), or Add to conversation, only adds your message; nobody replies until you ask.
  • Shift-Return or Option-Return starts a new line.
  • Speak, on a card, adds whatever you've typed and attached, then has that participant reply. With the box empty, it has that participant respond to the conversation as it stands: the way to have one participant answer another.

The note beside the button always shows what Return will do ("↩ adds, then Grok replies · ⌘↩ just adds") and changes as you type. With the box empty, Return does nothing, so a stray keypress never pays for a reply.

Choosing who replies

End your message with Example What happens
a name "…what do you think, Grok?" Grok replies.
several names "…, Gemini and Claude?" Each replies once, in that order, seeing the replies before it.
names and "independently" "…, Claude and Grok, independently" They reply at once, without seeing each other's replies.
"team" "…what do you think, team?" Every awake participant replies once, in turn.
"team" and "independently" "…team, independently" Every awake participant replies at once, without seeing the others' replies.
"privately" "…, Grok, privately" As without "privately", but only those named see it (see Private messages).
"only" "…, Grok only" The same as "privately", right after a name, names or "team".
your own name "…Grok always says this, Bill." No participant sees it: an aside (see Asides to the readers).
nothing of these "…that's my view." Whoever spoke last replies.
  • These words are for Dialog, not part of your message. Dialog takes them off ("What do you think, Grok?" becomes "What do you think?") and marks your message with a stage direction instead: "(to Grok)", "(to everyone, in turn)". The participants are told the same in plain words, so everyone knows whom you asked.
  • Only the last word counts, so "I agree with Claude." asks Claude. Case and punctuation don't matter, and the longest matching name wins ("…Claude 2" asks Claude 2). Separate several names with spaces, commas, "and", "&", "+" or "/".
  • "parallel" and "in parallel" mean the same as "independently", before or after the names. Since they are ordinary English too, "…can these run in parallel, team?" starts an independent round; the note beside the button shows this before you press Return.
  • Small typing slips are forgiven ("tema", "independantly", "paralel", "privatly"), but real words that are close are not: "…in the long term." starts no round.
  • A misspelled name ("…what do you think, Grk?") gets a question: "Did you mean Grok?" OK reads it as the name; Cancel sends your message as you wrote it. You're only asked when the name would count: not while others are replying, or when ⌘↩ just adds your message (unless it ends with "privately").
  • With no name at the end, Return asks the last participant who spoke openly and is awake. If nobody has spoken yet, Return only adds your message.

Rounds

  • In turn ("…, team", or several names): the participants reply one after another, each seeing the replies before it. A "team" round goes in card order, starting after the last speaker, so nobody speaks twice in a row. Hover over the note beside the button for the order.
  • Independently ("…team, independently"): every request goes out at once, and the replies fill in side by side, in their places. Nobody sees the others' replies from that round, which keeps everyone from following whoever answered first; afterwards, everyone sees them all. The round takes about as long as its slowest participant. Its replies are marked "(answering independently)".
  • Sleeping participants sit "team" rounds out. If everyone is asleep, your message is just added.
  • In a round taken in turn, Stop ends the current reply and the rest of the round. In an independent round, each card's Stop ends only that reply. A failed reply is skipped and the round goes on.
  • A round costs about as much as all its replies together.

Private messages

  • "…, privately" at the very end of your message, or To: Privately: <name> in the menu beside the button, sends the message to that participant only, and it answers at once. While a message will be private, the box turns parchment-colored and the button reads "Add privately" or "Send privately to <name>".
  • Several names make a private group: "…, Claude and Grok, privately" goes to both, who reply in turn (or at once, with "independently") and see each other's replies. "…, team, privately" goes to every awake participant; sleeping ones never see it.
  • "only" works like "privately", but only right after a name, names or "team" ("…what do you really think, Grok only?"). Elsewhere it is an ordinary word: "Answer in one sentence only" is an ordinary message.
  • With no name, a private message goes to whoever spoke last; with a card's Speak, to that card's participant. ⌘↩ adds it privately without asking for a reply.
  • Private exchanges are set on parchment and marked "(privately, to Grok)". No one outside them ever sees them. The participants in them remember them, as they would in their own chat window, and a private exchange doesn't change who is next.
  • Nothing goes to everyone by mistake: if Dialog can't tell whom a private message is for, the button waits and the note says why.

Asides to the readers

An aside is a comment for whoever reads the conversation later, yourself included: what you were thinking, why you asked what you did, a note on a reply. It stays in the conversation and in saved files, but no participant ever sees it, and no one replies to it.

  • End your message with your own name (the one in Settings): "Readers: Grok always says this, Bill." Your name comes off, as a participant's would. A participant with the same name as yours wins, and a message that is just your name is an ordinary message.
  • Or choose To: Aside: <your name> in the menu beside the button; then whatever you type is an aside, however it ends.
  • While a message will be an aside, the box shows it in italics, the button reads "Add aside", and Return, ⌘↩ and the button all just add it. You can add an aside at any time, even while participants are replying. A card's Speak adds it, then that participant replies to the conversation as before.
  • In the conversation, an aside is set in italics behind a dotted rule and marked "(aside, to the readers)", in the page, in saved pages and when copied into an email. A saved conversation attached to another one goes in without its asides.

Stage directions

What has been said never changes. If you change who is taking part or what they have been told, a line in its own style goes into the conversation at that point, with its time, as in a printed play:

  • (Gemini joins, using google/gemini-3-pro.)
  • (Grok leaves the conversation.)
  • (Grok is now called Skeptic.)
  • (Claude's model changes from one model to another.)
  • (Claude's thinking level changes from high to max.)
  • (Grok goes to sleep.)
  • (Grok wakes and reviews the entire conversation so far.)
  • (Bill changes the house rules.)
  • (Bill changes what everyone is told about Bill.)
  • (Bill changes Grok's role; only Grok sees this.)

Who sees them. You and every participant, except that a change of role is seen only by you and that participant. A sleeping participant catches up when it wakes.

The full change. When the line doesn't say it all (the house rules, About you, a role), it has a triangle: click it to see what the changed lines said before and say now. The participants are sent the new text in full.

A sleeping participant you ask by name wakes for that reply and goes back to sleep after it, with a line before and a line after. In a private exchange those lines are private too. If the reply fails, its wake line goes away.

When a line appears. When you finish the change: when you leave the Role or name box, close Settings, or choose from a menu. Until someone next speaks, you can change your mind: put a participant to sleep and wake it again and no line is left; retype a name and there's one line for the name you ended with. Once a turn follows, a line stays, and it can't be removed.

Nothing is reported before the conversation begins: everyone starts with the settings as they are at the first turn. A participant who joins later starts with the settings as they are then, and sees the conversation from the beginning. Reordering the cards isn't a change. Lines are saved and copied along with the turns, and can be ticked for Save Selected.

Reading replies

  • Replies appear as they are written. Until something arrives, "waiting for Claude… 12 s" shows; the request may be queued at the host or searching the web. Replies are formatted (headings, lists, tables, code), and links open in a new tab. A wide table uses the free space to the right of the text, so widening the window gives it more room; if it still doesn't fit, it scrolls sideways.
  • The page follows a reply while you're at the bottom. Scroll up to read something earlier and it stays put until you scroll back down.
  • Thinking. When a model sends its thinking, a folded Thinking line sits above its reply: click to read it. Some models send all of it, many (Claude, Gemini) a summary, and some (OpenAI's) none. Thinking is kept only while the page is open, is never saved, and no participant ever sees it.
  • Mathematics in LaTeX, as models often write it, is shown as mathematics once the reply is complete. Prices such as "$5 and $10" stay money. LaTeX that can't be read is shown in red; point at it for the reason.
  • The margin of each turn shows a tick box for saving, the time, the speaker's name and, for a reply, its cost and speed. After the text come any sources used, and "stopped early" if you stopped it.
  • Participants see the times too: every turn reaches them with the time it began (the date as well on the first turn of each day), their own earlier replies included, so you can point one to a turn by its time ("what you said at 17:12").
  • Click a reply's cost or speed for its particulars: the exact model that answered and its thinking level, the host (the company whose servers ran it), the cost table and the speed's breakdown. Click again to hide them.
  • The cost table splits the cost into reading (the conversation it was given, including how much was re-read at the discount and how much was stored for re-reading), writing (including thinking) and, when it used the web, searches and pages read. OpenRouter reports the total; when it doesn't report the parts, Dialog works them out from the model's listed prices and marks them "about". Amounts are to the cent.
  • Speed is the tokens the model wrote (thinking included) per second, from pressing Speak to the last word. It includes thinking, web searches and network delay, so it measures how fast the participant answers you. In the particulars, under "Speed:", a small table lists the total time, the time to the first word, tokens per second overall, and tokens per second since the first word.
  • × on a turn (it appears when you hover) removes it, so no participant sees it again.
  • A stopped reply keeps what arrived, and the others see it.
  • When a reply fails, Dialog shows OpenRouter's message and whatever details it sent (which host failed, the request id). Try again replaces it with a fresh attempt. Failed replies are never shown to any participant.
  • When a model is withdrawn from OpenRouter, its card says so and its replies fail; choose another model with the seat's model button. The seat keeps its name, role and earlier turns. Ids beginning with "~" follow the newest version and don't go away.

Web search and reading pages

Every participant whose model can use tools may search the web and read web pages, deciding for itself when. OpenRouter does both on its behalf; there is nothing to set.

  • A search costs about 1 to 3 cents. Claude and some others stop at 10 searches a reply. Sources used are listed under the reply.
  • Reading a page: a participant can read any web page or online PDF whose address comes up in the conversation. It costs very little, plus the page's text at the model's input price, which for a long page and an expensive model can be 10 to 30 cents.
  • While a participant searches or reads, its reply simply pauses. The cost table shows how many searches and pages a reply used.
  • Only the reader sees the page; the others see what it says about it. Each reading is paid once, which is usually far cheaper than attaching the page as a file, since an attached file is re-read by every participant every turn. Attach it instead (save it as a PDF and drag it in) when everyone should work from the same copy, or when a site won't let OpenRouter in.

Files

Attaching files

Drag files onto the conversation, or click Attach…. They wait above the input box (× drops one) and go out with your next message, privately if the message is private.

  • Text files (Markdown, plain text, CSV, code and the like) go to every model as text. In the conversation, click the triangle by a text file's name to see what's in it (and again to fold it away), as in a saved conversation.
  • A conversation saved by Dialog (Save All or Save Selected) goes in as just its turns, as plain text, named "… (transcript).txt": each turn's time (with the date on the first turn of each day), speaker and model, private exchanges marked as private, and the names of any files that were attached or made, but not the files themselves, nor your asides to the readers. That is far smaller than the saved page, which also holds its appearance and the data Restore… uses. To carry on with the conversation itself, use Restore… instead.
  • Images (PNG, JPEG, WebP, GIF) are scaled down if large. A model that can't view images is told one was shared. A model can misread an image: to check, ask about a detail only the picture shows.
  • PDFs go to models that read them themselves; for others, OpenRouter converts them to text free. Models in LM Studio get no PDFs: attach the text instead.
  • Word, Pages, Excel and similar files can't be read; export them as PDF.
  • Cost: an attached file is re-read by every participant every time it speaks. Text files show their size in tokens, and Dialog asks before attaching one over 50,000 tokens.
  • Browsers give a page only a few megabytes of storage, so a very large file may be marked "not saved": it lasts until the page closes. Removing its turn frees the space.

Files the participants make

A participant can hand you a file: a document, a table, a drawing, a small web page (a calculator, a chart, a game). Ask for one ("…put that in a Markdown file, Claude"), or a participant may offer one.

  • Kinds: Markdown (.md), plain text (.txt, .csv and the like), drawings (.svg) and web pages (.html).
  • Each file is a card under its reply, with Open (in a new tab: allow pop-ups if nothing opens), Save… and × (remove it, so no participant sees it again).
  • The others see the files, and can comment on them or improve them. A participant revises a file by writing it again under the same name; the newer version replaces the older one for everyone. A file in a private reply stays private.
  • Cost: like an attached file, it is re-read by every participant every turn until it is removed or replaced. Removing files nobody needs saves money.
  • Safety: a web page a model wrote could hold anything, so Open shows it walled off from Dialog, with no way to reach your keys or your conversation. A saved copy has no such wall: open it by double-clicking only if you've read it or trust it as you would any download.

Saving, restoring and tabs

  • Save All saves the whole conversation as one web page, readable in any browser: the participants, one line each with the name and the first sentence of the role, and a triangle that opens the model, thinking level and whole role. The list shows everyone as they were at the start of the conversation, or on joining if they came later, and that includes anyone since removed; stage directions show later changes, and a reply given with another model or thinking level is marked with the one it used. (A conversation begun on an earlier version of Dialog lists everyone as of the time of saving, and says so.) Then every turn with its time (the date as well on the first turn of each day), private exchanges and asides included and marked; attached images and files; the participants' files; sources; and each reply's cost and speed. Failed replies and thinking are left out.

  • Save Selected: tick the box beside some turns (it shows when you point at a turn) and the button becomes Save Selected, which saves just those turns.

  • Restore… brings a conversation back. It appears in the top bar while the conversation is empty (after New conversation, say) and lists, newest first, one line each with when it was saved and the first sentence of your first message:

    • the last 25 conversations, kept in this browser, a safety net for the ones you didn't save. Dialog keeps them with their files, updating each one as it goes, so a conversation survives closing its tab, quitting the browser or pressing New conversation. The × at the end of one deletes it from the browser, after asking. It's a net, not an archive: clearing your browser's website data removes them, and Safari removes them by itself if you don't open Dialog for about a week of using Safari (unless Dialog is added to the Dock or Home Screen, see below). Save anything you want to keep.
    • the conversations in the files and folders you add with Add from… at the foot of the list: a conversation saved with Save All or Save Selected, or a folder of them (in a folder, only the conversations in the folder itself are listed, not in folders inside it). Each folder or file you add joins those already listed, and they are all read again each time Restore… opens. In Safari you can add a folder or a single file; Safari forgets them when the page closes, and when it reads a folder again it sees only the files that were there when you added it, not conversations saved into it since (add the folder again for those). In Chrome you add folders, not single files; its folder chooser opens where you last saved, and Chrome remembers the folders you add: from then on they are read every time, new conversations included, after Chrome asks once a visit whether Dialog may look in them.

    The ↑ and ↓ keys move through the list; Return chooses. Dialog asks before resuming one; Cancel takes you back to the list. Choose one and carry on where it left off. Everything comes back as it was, including the participants, house rules, your name and About you. Your keys are never in a saved conversation, so yours stay.

  • Where files go: Chrome lets you choose the folder and name. Safari saves to Downloads, unless you set Safari Settings › General › File download location to "Ask for each download".

  • Copying into an email: select part of the conversation (or all of it with ⌘A), copy and paste into Mail: it looks as it does on screen, without buttons, costs or speeds.

  • New conversation clears the conversation; participants and settings stay. Save first if you want to keep it. ⌘-click it (Ctrl-click on other systems) to start a new conversation in a new tab instead, leaving this one as it is.

  • Each tab is a conversation of its own, with its own participants and settings. A new tab starts empty, with the participants and settings as you last changed them in any tab.

  • A tab keeps its conversation when you reload it. Closing the tab (or quitting the browser) ends the conversation without asking; Restore… can still bring it back.

  • On an iPad or iPhone, Dialog works the same in Safari. Adding it to the Home Screen (Share › Add to Home Screen), or on a Mac to the Dock (File › Add to Dock), gives it a window of its own and keeps the conversations kept for Restore… safe from Safari's one-week clean-up. It also keeps its own settings: bring yours across with Save settings to a file and Restore from a file.

Settings

  • OpenRouter API key, with Show to check what you pasted.
  • OpenRouter management key (optional), used only to show your remaining credit in the top bar. Create one at openrouter.ai/settings/management-keys, with an expiration date. Be aware that a management key can create new keys: someone who got hold of it could spend your remaining credit.
  • LM Studio server (optional): see Models on this computer, below.
  • Your name: what the participants call you (Grasshopper until you change it).
  • About you: a few sentences every participant starts with, after the house rules and before its role. Later changes reach them as a stage direction.
  • House rules: the instructions every participant starts with; later changes reach them as a stage direction, and only today's date keeps up by itself. The defaults explain how the conversation works and ask for brief, honest, careful contributions: no flattery, confidence given as a percentage, alternatives shown with the evidence against them, and a qualified answer preferred to a confident mistake. Edit freely; Restore default rules undoes your edits. These placeholders are filled in for each participant: {name} its name, {humanName} your name, {participants} everyone present with their models, {date} today's date with your offset from UTC ("2026 Oct 27 (UTC-7)"). After the rules Dialog adds a short note of its own, on how to make files, which you can't edit.
  • Save settings to a file… saves your name, About you, the house rules and every participant (name, model, thinking level, role, color, asleep or not), as a backup or to move to another browser. Include my keys in the saved file adds your keys; keep such a file private.
  • Restore from a file… reads such a file and asks before replacing your settings (and your keys, if the file has them). The conversation isn't changed.
  • Done, Escape or a click outside closes Settings. Changes are kept as you type.

Models on this computer (LM Studio)

A participant can use a model running in LM Studio on your own computer. It costs nothing, and its messages never leave the computer. LM Studio and OpenRouter participants can share a conversation.

Setting up:

  1. In LM Studio, download a model and load it.
  2. Start LM Studio's server with CORS on, which lets a web page talk to it. In Terminal: ~/.lmstudio/bin/lms server start --cors While CORS is on, any website you visit in this computer's browsers can use the server, so stop it when you're done: ~/.lmstudio/bin/lms server stop.
  3. In Settings, click the LM Studio server box: it fills in the usual address, http://localhost:1234.
  4. Press a seat's model button: LM Studio's models come first, marked "(LM Studio)", with "loaded" beside those loaded now. One not loaded is loaded when first asked, which can take a while.

Which browser: Chrome works with the online Dialog; allow it to reach devices on your local network when it asks. Safari works only with a copy of index.html opened from your disk. That copy keeps its own settings; Save settings to a file and Restore from a file carry them across.

What's different:

  • No cost is shown, and there is no web search or page reading.
  • The Thinking setting is fixed at the model's default.
  • Images go only to models LM Studio lists as able to view them; PDFs aren't sent.
  • The conversation must fit the model's context length, which LM Studio sets when it loads the model (often 8,192 tokens). A longer conversation fails with a message saying how many tokens it needs: load the model again with a larger context length.
  • If Dialog can't reach LM Studio, check that the server is running with CORS on and that the address in Settings is right.

How it works, and what it costs

Each participant starts from scratch every turn. The models keep no memory between requests. Each time a participant speaks, Dialog sends its instructions as they were when it joined (house rules, About you, its role) and the whole conversation as that participant sees it, stage directions included. Two seats running the same model share nothing but the transcript; each is told every participant's model.

No hidden instructions from the makers' apps. The ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini apps give their models long hidden instructions about tone, formatting and safety. Here a model gets only what Dialog sends, so it may sound noticeably different than in its maker's app.

One key, many hosts. Every request goes through your OpenRouter key; OpenRouter passes it to a company that runs the model (Anthropic itself, say, or Amazon's or Google's cloud). The model's makers never see your key.

The re-reading discount. Since every turn re-sends the whole conversation, the hosts charge much less for text they've seen recently: for Claude Opus 5.5, a re-read costs a twentieth of a fresh read. Dialog asks Claude for its 1-hour cache, which costs extra to fill but soon pays for itself. Since a change reaches a participant as a stage direction, what it has already read stays the same and the discount carries on. Only a new day, which changes the date in its instructions, means its next turn reads everything at full price once. Long conversations and attached files are what make a conversation expensive; start a new one when the old one has served its purpose.

Where your data lives

The conversation, files, settings and keys are kept in your browser, on this computer only. Each tab keeps its own conversation until it closes; your latest settings are kept for the next tab, and your last 25 conversations for Restore…. Each browser keeps its own: Chrome doesn't see what Safari has. Use Save settings to a file and Save All to carry things across.

Messages go only to OpenRouter, which passes each to the model you chose, or, for an LM Studio participant, only to LM Studio on this computer. The management key goes only to OpenRouter's credit check. The makers' logos are fetched from OpenRouter's site (and for a few makers from Google's icon service); those requests carry nothing of yours.
